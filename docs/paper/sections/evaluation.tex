\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We measured the operations whose cost determines whether the design is practical on ordinary hardware, using the reference implementation. All numbers are from one machine (Apple M2 Max laptop, 32~GB, macOS 26.7, Rust 1.94 release builds, Node.js 24.6), averaged over the stated number of repetitions after a warm-up. They are microbenchmarks of local computation; we did not measure anything over a network, Tor, or a phone. Table~\ref{tab:measure} lists them.

The share size follows from the format: one 33-byte point, 256 ciphertext pairs of two 33-byte points each, 256 OR-proofs of four 32-byte scalars each, and a 64-byte DLEQ proof: $33+256\cdot(66+128)+64=49{,}761$. An escrow funding exchange carries two shares (one per dispute branch), so about 100~KB, and a dispute letter carries one. Decryption tests each of the 256 bits against the identity and $G$; because the moderator verifies a letter once and caches the result, decryption costs a few milliseconds rather than a second full verification.

These numbers support three conclusions. Signing and verifying the catalog and sealing orders cost well under a millisecond each, so they are not a bottleneck. On-device search over tens of thousands of headers is interactive on a laptop; we have not measured it on a phone. The verifiable share dominates the escrow's cost in both bytes and time, which is why smaller proofs are the first item of future work (\S\ref{sec:future}).
